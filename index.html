Concept dossier · working draft

The Reuse Layer

Sorting technology was built to answer one question: what is this made of? The market has moved on to a harder one: who would buy this, for how much, and in what condition. Answering it per item is now a shipping product sold for two cents. Turning the answer into a record a regulator will accept is not.

01 · Framing

The question the machines were built to answer is no longer the question being asked

Every sorting line in Europe was designed around composition. Near-infrared sensors, hyperspectral cameras, fibre identification: the whole apparatus exists to route a garment toward the right recycler. It is good technology aimed at a market that has not arrived.

Roughly 1% of European textile waste is recycled back into new textiles. Meanwhile the second-hand market is where the actual money and the actual volume sit, and it is growing faster than the apparel market as a whole. The economic decision that matters for almost every garment is not which recycler but can this be worn again, by whom, and at what price.

That decision is made today by a person holding the garment, in a few seconds, using judgement they cannot articulate and nobody can audit. It does not scale, it cannot be reported on, and it is about to become a legal obligation to measure.

The pressure is already visible

Soex filed for insolvency in October 2024. Texaid Deutschland followed in June 2025. Le Relais suspended collection in France mid-2025. The Boston Consulting Group's assessment of the sector is titled Textile Waste at a Tipping Point. The sorting industry is failing not because it cannot identify fibres, but because it cannot extract enough value per item to cover the cost of handling it.

A technology that raises the realised value of the reusable fraction is not a nice-to-have for this sector. It is the difference between a viable business and an insolvency filing.

02 · The core insight

Condition barely moves the price. Identity and desirability do.

Fashion for Good and Circle Economy examined 8,280 post-consumer garments at sorting facilities in the Netherlands, Spain, Poland and Lithuania. The finding is the single most useful thing in this document, because it contradicts the obvious product spec.

Physical condition showed almost no correlation with resale price. What set the grade was brand recognition, current fashion trends, and perceived desirability. Sorters price the label, not the fabric.

Three consequences follow, and they should shape the entire product:

  • Brand and model recognition is the primary capability, not defect detection. If the model cannot read a worn, faded, half-cut label, it cannot price the item. Note that 16% of the sample had no identifiable brand at all: a garment nobody can name is a garment nobody can price.
  • Defect detection is the second capability, and its job is upgrade, not rejection. The commercial question is not "is this damaged" but "is this a stain that will wash out, a seam that costs €3 to fix, or a hole that ends the garment's commercial life". That is a repairability model, and it is where the 20-point gap lives.
  • A price is a demand prediction, not a condition score. The model needs comparable sold prices, channel, region and season. This makes marketplace data a strategic input, and it is the part of the system that is hardest for a competitor to copy.
Where existing research does not help you

The published literature on automated garment inspection is almost entirely about new fabric on a production line: roll defects, seam faults, factory quality control. Worn garments are a different problem, with different lighting, deformation, and failure modes, and there is no meaningful public dataset. That absence is your moat and your first bottleneck at the same time.

03 · The ecosystem

One assessment layer, three supply routes, four exits

The venture is not a marketplace and not a sorting machine. It is the layer in between: the thing that turns a physical garment into a priced, described, listable, reportable record. Everything else in the diagram already exists and already has owners.

Read the diagram as two businesses stacked on one pipeline. The top half is a per-item operational service sold on cost and throughput. The bottom half is a data and compliance product sold on insight and reporting. The first funds the second; the second is where the defensibility and the margin live. This is structurally the same shape as Texfile's thesis, pointed at reuse instead of recycling.

04 · The product

Five stages, and the one that is genuinely hard

Stage 1

Capture

Hanger rig with fixed lighting for operators; guided phone capture for individuals. Both must produce comparable images, or the model sees two different worlds. Solved by hardware that already exists.

Stage 2

Identify

Brand, model, size, fibre composition, colour, category, era, original retail price. Mostly a vision-language problem on labels and silhouettes. This is the closest thing to Texfile's existing capability.

Stage 3

Assess

Defect type, severity and location, plus the commercial verdict: washable, repairable at what cost, or terminal. Vendors already flag damage; attaching a cost and a decision to it is the part still open, and no public dataset exists for it.

Stage 4

Value

Price, best channel, expected days-to-sell. A demand model fed by comparable sold prices, region and season. Needs a live data feed, not a one-off training set.

Stage 5

Publish

Clean photo set, measurements, description, translations, feed into the destination channel. Commoditising fast, so treat it as table stakes rather than differentiation.

Stage 6

Learn

Every sale, every unsold item, every returned item is a label. The loop from realised price back to the model is the reason this gets better with volume and a competitor's cold start does not.

Be honest with yourself about where the difficulty is. Stages 1, 2 and 5 are engineering with known solutions and shrinking margins. Stages 3, 4 and 6 are where a company can be built, and all three of them are data problems before they are model problems.

Note what is deliberately absent: sensor-based composition analysis. Near-infrared and hyperspectral hardware is expensive, is aimed at the recycling decision, and adds little to a reuse price that is set by the label on the collar. Leave that to the sorting-machine vendors and stay a software layer.

The output record

Every item should exit the pipeline as one structured record: identity, condition, repair verdict, recommended route, price, channel, confidence per field, and a link to its passport. The confidence scores matter more than they sound. They are what lets an operator send only the uncertain 15% to a human, which is the only realistic path to a deployment they will actually accept.

06 · Market and timing

A growing market, a collapsing supply chain, and a regulatory deadline

$393BGlobal second-hand apparel by 2030, ~10% of all apparel spend (ThredUp)
€26BEuropean second-hand fashion by 2030, from €15.9B in 2024 (CFF / KPMG)
€10.8BVinted GMV in 2025, up 47% year on year
81%Share of EU second-hand purchases made in physical stores, not online

Two of these numbers should change how you think about the product. The first is Vinted: a single European platform now moves more merchandise value than the entire branded resale-as-a-service sector, which tells you where the listing volume is. The second is the 81%. Most European second-hand still sells over a counter, so a product that only produces online listings addresses under a fifth of the market. In-store pricing, routing and shelf-life decisions are not a later feature; they are half the business.

Why now, specifically

The Netherlands shows what "reported number" means in practice: producers must already hit 20% reuse in 2025, rising to 25% by 2030, with a portion required to happen domestically, and they must report annually. Once a company has to publish a reuse percentage, it needs a per-item, auditable method of establishing that a garment was actually reusable. That is precisely what your assessment record is.

The counterweight is the sector's finances. Grade B garments sell for €0.45 to €2.10 per kilo. Whatever you charge per item has to be small enough to disappear inside that, or large enough to be justified by a demonstrated uplift in realised price. There is no third option, and you should decide which one you are selling before you build anything.

The gap between what the market is buying and what it is about to need

Put every one of these vendors side by side and the same pattern appears. All of them optimise throughput: getting an item from a bin to a listing faster and cheaper than a person could. That is a legitimate business and it has been thoroughly contested. It also has a floor, and Enhancy has already found it at roughly two cents an item. Competing there means arriving late to a price war over a service somebody already ships.

What none of them produce is the thing the next three years actually require: a per-item record of what was reusable, why, and what became of it, in a form a brand, an EPR organisation or a passport registry will accept. Look across the matrix below and the left-hand columns are crowded while the right-hand columns are almost empty. The only actor touching that side is a research consortium that runs until 2029.

The reason for the emptiness is commercial, not technical. The buyer for throughput is a sorting operator with no money, and the vendor economics show it: a company with Emmaüs and Le Relais as reference customers reported turnover on the order of €50,000 in its fourth year. The buyer for the record is a brand or an eco-organisation with a legal obligation and a budget, and that buyer does not exist yet. It arrives with the EPR schemes.

So the position is this. Treat throughput as commodity infrastructure, and compete where these companies are absent. Match the intake pipeline well enough to be credible, buy the parts that are genuinely hardware, and put the differentiation in four things nobody in this table sells today: a repair verdict with a cost attached rather than a damage flag; an auditable reuse record wired into the passport; insight flowing back to producers and designers about what actually survives; and a routing decision that includes the shop floor, not only the listing feed.

The gap, stated plainly

Intake automation is a solved and contested problem with a two-cent price tag. Turning intake into an auditable reuse record that a brand or an EPR organisation can report against, and into insight a producer and a designer can act on, is neither solved nor contested. One is a commodity with an incumbent. The other is a regulated asset with a 2028 deadline and no incumbent.

08 · Sequencing

All three customers, in this order and for these reasons

Sorters and resale operators

They already measure cost per item and seconds per item, so your value is arithmetic rather than persuasion. They are under acute financial pressure, which shortens sales cycles. And they hold the garments you need to build the dataset.

Risk: they have no money. Price it as a share of uplift, not a licence fee.

Brands and marketplaces

Take-back and branded resale programmes have budget, a compliance deadline, and a reason to want per-SKU reuse analytics. They also validate the data product, which is the higher-margin half of the business.

Risk: long procurement, pilot purgatory. Enter through the resale-as-a-service providers who already hold the contract.

Individuals

The hanger-scan consumer flow you described. Highest volume, best story, and the source of the provenance data that makes the passport genuinely useful.

Risk: acquisition cost and platform competition. Reach consumers through an operator or marketplace partner, not directly.

The order matters because each phase pays for the next one's hardest input. Operators give you the training data. Brands give you the revenue and the compliance wedge. Consumers give you the volume and the provenance, but only once the model is good enough that a wrong price does not cost you the user.

Reuse data & compliance recordsBrands, EPR organisations, municipalitiesSubscription, or per certified recordThe real business. Regulation-driven, high margin, defensible, and it compounds with every item you touch.

What the competitor landscape shows should change the emphasis here rather than the structure. Lines one and two are a commodity with an incumbent at two cents an item, and a company built on them reached roughly €50k of turnover in four years. Lines three and four are what you actually sell. Treat the per-item service as customer acquisition and data collection priced near cost, not as a revenue line.

If you want one sentence for a funder: garment intake is being automated by others and we will match it; what nobody sells is the verified reuse record that comes out the other end, and from 2028 every brand in Europe has to report one.

On funding

The routes worth evaluating on their own merits: EIC Accelerator and Pathfinder, Eurostars, EIT Manufacturing and EIT Climate-KIC, Interreg, regional instruments in Bremen, and the textile-specific Eurocluster calls. Each has its own fit and its own timing; judge each against what this venture will look like at the point of submission rather than against each other.

Note one structural advantage you already hold. A spin-off with access to a sorting consortium's operational sites can assemble a labelled dataset of worn garments, with repair verdicts and realised prices attached, that a better-funded competitor cannot simply buy. Protect that access contractually before you need it.

10 · Risks

What could make this not work

  • The operational layer is already commoditised. Enhancy sells the scan-tag-price-list pipeline for a claimed two cents an item. Any plan whose core is "we automate garment intake" is competing on price against a shipping incumbent from a standing start. The plan has to lead with the record, not the pipeline.
  • The buyers may simply not pay. Enhancy's reported turnover of roughly €50k in 2023, with Emmaüs and Le Relais as reference customers, is the clearest signal in this document about willingness to pay. Reference logos in this sector do not convert into revenue. Assume the same about yours until a contract says otherwise, and note that Le Relais suspended collection in 2025.
  • Unit economics. Grade B stock trades at under €2 a kilo. If your per-item cost cannot get to a few cents, the only viable pitch is measured uplift, and that pitch requires a controlled pricing experiment to prove it.
  • Brand recognition is the bottleneck. If price is set by the label and 16% of garments have no readable label, your accuracy ceiling on the highest-value task may be lower than a demo suggests. Measure this before you promise it.
  • The offline majority. 81% of European second-hand sells in physical shops. A product that only produces online listings is addressing a minority of the market and will hit a ceiling.
  • Platform encroachment. eBay and Vinted are already shipping photo-to-listing. Assume Stage 5 has no long-term value and that Stage 2 partly commoditises too.
  • TexMat overlap. A €6M consortium is working adjacent ground until 2029, and it sits inside the same EU project ecosystem you already know. Decide early whether that is a partnership, a differentiation exercise, or a reason to narrow scope.
  • Data rights. Garment images taken at partner sites, resale price data, and passport records all have ownership questions. Settle them in writing at the pilot stage, not at the funding stage.
  • The relationship to Texfile. A spin-off sharing founders, mentors and consortium access with an existing venture needs a clear line on IP, data and exploitation rights. This is the same issue Texfile has already had to resolve, so the precedent exists.
